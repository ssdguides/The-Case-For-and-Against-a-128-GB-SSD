A 128 GB SSD costs so little that it’s tempting to buy one for everything. Whether it’s a good buy depends entirely on the job.

It works well as a revival. If an older laptop or desktop still boots from a spinning hard disk, replacing it with any SSD is usually the most noticeable upgrade you can make. Startup, app launches and general responsiveness all improve.

It works as a boot drive. Put Windows and core apps on the SSD and keep files and media on a larger drive.

It works for fixed-purpose machines. Point-of-sale terminals, kiosks, thin clients and lab PCs run a set group of programs and rarely need much space.

It doesn’t work for gaming or creative work. Modern games can take tens of gigabytes each, and raw photos, 4K footage and project caches fill a small drive quickly.

Before buying, understand the space math. A “128 GB” drive appears as roughly 119 GB in Windows. After Windows, updates and system files, many people have around 80–90 GB. Leave 10–20% free and you’re working with considerably less.

A simple test from the guide: add up what you want on the drive today. If the total is above roughly 80 GB, go up a size.

Finally, don’t skip compatibility. A 2.5-inch SATA drive, an M.2 SATA stick, an M.2 NVMe drive and an mSATA card are not interchangeable, and an M.2 drive that physically fits may still fail if the slot doesn’t support that interface. Check your device manual first.
